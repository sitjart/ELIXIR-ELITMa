Indicators: providing evidence to strengthen your impact case

Indicators serve as proof to substantiate the connections between activities, outcomes and impact areas. Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) are the primary evidence used to demonstrate the strength of these relationships. The more evidence you collect, the more justified your claims about your impact become. While KPIs are often quantitative, they can also be qualitative, provided the data collection methodology is systematic and evidence-based.
Status
in development
Primary audience
Head of Nodes, Nodes Coordinators, Impact officers, Communication officers, Technical coordinators, Platform leads, Product managers, Service managers
  1. Understand what indicators are and how to design them
  2. Integrate indicators into your impact logic.
  3. Identify “good enough” indicators as nothing is perfect

Key principles

  1. Proportionality and the “Sweet Spot” (★): Developing indicators can be labor-intensive, particularly during the monitoring phase (far more so than designing them). Strive for proportionality—ensure the effort invested in monitoring an indicator (or developing a new one) is justified by the added value it provides. If the effort outweighs the benefits, reevaluate your approach. Also remember that frequency of data collection matters; too frequent collection and you may miss meaningful trends; too infrequent and you might overlook critical peaks or drops in your impact narrative.

    Tip: Leverage existing methodologies developed by others. If multiple teams within ELIXIR use the same methodology, you can aggregate data with them in the future to gain broader insights.

  2. Differentiating Impact and Performance: There should be indicators along the impact pathway.
    1. Activity Indicators: Measure the scale and nature of activities. These are typically part of internal reporting.
    2. Outcome Indicators: Document the results of the first productive round of interactions with involved stakeholders.
    3. Impact Indicators: Reflect the extent and nature of effects on the economy and society. If it is not possible to collect data, then these can be estimations.

    Indicators can relate to impact (sphere of interest) or performance (spheres of control and influence), but the boundary between the two isn’t always clear as some activity-level indicators can already highlight effects on impact areas.

  3. Evolution of Indicators: Not all indicators are equally mature. Some, like those in scientometrics, are well-established, while others (e.g. economic impact indicators) remain underdeveloped due to methodological challenges. Stay updated through the Impact Group, literature, or conferences, and experiment with new approaches.

When developing indicators:

  • Identify the source of information, analytical methodology and final product for your audience.
  • Indicators don’t need to be perfect—acknowledge the strengths and weaknesses of your data and methodology. As long as contributors and managers agree on the narrative, you’re on the right track.
  • Continuously refine your methodology by collecting data over time to identify further strengths and weaknesses.

If resources allow:

  • Document metadata for each indicator (e.g. description, methodology, limitations, references).
  • Establish baselines and targets to avoid excessive analysis.
  • Combine quantitative and qualitative indicators to build a robust case.

Quantitative indicators

Quantitative indicators measure quantities (e.g. numbers, indices, percentages, ratios) based on available monitoring data.

Differentiate between:

  • Indicators (e.g. citation impact),
  • Metrics (e.g. average citations, total citations, highly cited publications),
  • Measurements (e.g. data sources used for metrics).

The table captures examples of indicators, along with their strengths and weaknesses which can give you an idea how to evaluate your decisions:

Indicator Method Strengths Weaknesses
User Count IP addresses Live usage data, large numbers, geographical/institutional insights (e.g. academia vs. industry). GDPR restrictions, multiple IPs per user inflate numbers.
  Webpage visits Live usage data, large numbers. AI tools (e.g. ChatGPT) may bypass visits; limited user analysis.
  Mailing lists Accurate numbers, institutional/geographical analysis. Users may forget to unsubscribe, change jobs/emails or mark emails as spam.
Publication Count (e.g. of ELIXIR related publications) Text-mining for ELIXIR acknowledgments Accurate count of ELIXIR-supported publications; gender/geographical analysis. ELIXIR term used beyond the infrastructure; manual validation required.
  Text-mining for EU-funded ELIXIR projects Accurate count for ELIXIR network; authorship/affiliation analysis. Potential acknowledgment errors; requires updates on EU-funded projects’ numbers and not project names.
Citation Count (e.g. of ELIXIR related publications) Total citation counts Aggregated data shows progress over time; real usage in building new science; possible field of science analysis. Not all users publish (e.g. industry); aggregation masks highly cited publications.
  Average citation counts Baseline for comparison (e.g. ELIXIR vs. database averages). Most publications receive few/no citations; better to use median or quantiles.
Surveys for feedback Yes/No/I don’t know questions Easy to analyse; clear statements; effective for targeted audiences or conditional format. Limited depth; biased towards a group of people willing to respond; risk of misinterpretation without audience understanding.
  Multiple-choice answers Easy to analyse; shows trends; works for targeted audiences or conditional format Same as above: Still limited depth; biased; risk of misinterpretation.
  Open-ended questions Highly informative when combined with other question types. Difficult to analyse; low response rates.

Qualitative indicators

Qualitative indicators capture narrated information based on perceptions, meanings, and representations of stakeholders. These can be collected through case studies, surveys or in-depth interviews.

  • Best impact cases are captured through stories, narratives and testimonials.
  • Analysis Challenges: Qualitative data (e.g. testimonials) can be difficult to analyse. Tools like ChatGPT (for summarisation) or NVivo (specialized software) can help identify common themes.
  • Case studies are used to reflect the uniqueness and complexity of RIs better. Sometimes they show success stories, although this can lead to optimism bias and reproducibility.

When designing survey or interview questions, keep the following in mind:

  • Pilot your questions with a small group from your target audience. This helps refine or redefine your narrative and ensures clarity.
  • Include contextual information to help respondents understand the purpose and flow of the questions and ask for background information, as this might reveal unexpected insights or cases you hadn’t considered.
  • Recognise that contributors may lack the expertise or knowledge you expect, therefore adapt by focusing on high-level information (for interviews) or using conditional questions and skip logic (for surveys) to tailor the flow.
  • Predefine Analytical Methodology, if you anticipate a large volume of responses, establish a clear analytical framework in advance to ensure consistency, especially if multiple people will categorise information under specific indicators.

Exercise

Take a few minutes to practice on paper: create an impact pathway for a hypothetical scenario, such as developing a new recipe for the FAIRcookbook (or any other data management standard). Be sure to include indicators in your pathway. Identify up to 5 activities, 3 outputs, 3 outcomes, and 3 impact areas.
